## A trail is ice

An aircraft engine releases hot gases laden with water vapour, and that vapour is what makes the trail. As it mixes with air at {f.formation} °C or colder, the vapour condenses into droplets<SourceRef id="schumann1996" sources={sources} />. Particles from the engine, such as soot, act as seeds, then the droplets freeze<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

In dry air, the trail is gone within {f.shortMinutes} minutes. It only lasts if the air is supersaturated with respect to ice: it can then hold for up to {f.persistentHours} hours and spread into a veil of cirrus. Aircraft fly through such air {f.supersaturated} of the time<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

The same moist air, lifted by a low-pressure system, also makes natural cirrus<SourceRef id="spichtinger2005" sources={sources} /><SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />. That is why lasting trails and cirrus often show up together: they have the same cause.

</Step>

## Trails warm the climate

By day, a trail sends a little sunlight back to space. Day and night, it holds in the heat the Earth radiates, and at night nothing offsets it. Overall, the warming wins slightly<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

In {f.forcingYear}, contrails and their cirrus warmed the climate by about {f.contrailErf} milliwatts per square metre, within a range of {f.contrailErfRange}. That is more than the effect of the CO2 aviation has built up, {f.aviationCo2Erf} milliwatts per square metre<SourceRef id="lee2021" sources={sources} />. The authors rate this figure as uncertain, but its sign is not in doubt: spraying to cool the Earth that warms it would make no sense.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('forcing')} sources={sources} locale="en">

This ratio compares trails that last a few hours, renewed as long as planes fly, with a gas that builds up over centuries. It does not say which matters more in the long run.

</ShowCalc>

## What really cools: sulfur, very high up

In {f.pinatuboYear}, the Pinatubo volcano sent {f.pinatuboSo2} million tonnes of sulfur dioxide above {f.stratosphere} km<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />. The sulfur forms fine droplets of sulfuric acid there, which send part of the sunlight back. Up there, it takes about a year for their amount to drop by {f.aerosolDrop}; lower down, rain clears them within a few days<SourceRef id="pitari2002" sources={sources} />. The Earth’s surface cooled by about {f.cooling} °C<SourceRef id="canty2013" sources={sources} />, for {f.coolingYears} years<SourceRef id="robock2000" sources={sources} />.

Airliners fly at about {f.cruise} km<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />, well below that. Whatever they released would come down within days to months<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

## What a real programme would take

No existing aircraft climbs to about {f.stratosphere} km with enough load. Research aircraft get there, but with about {f.researchPayload} tonne. So one study designed a new aircraft able to carry {f.payload} tonnes of sulfur up there<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />.

In its scenario, the first year takes {f.so2Year1} million tonnes of SO2, {f.flightsYear1} flights and {f.aircraftYear1} aircraft. The fifteenth takes {f.so2Year15} million tonnes, {f.flightsYear15} flights and {f.aircraftYear15} aircraft. On average over the fifteen years, it costs about {f.cost} dollars a year. That is {f.pinatuboTimes1} times less sulfur than Pinatubo in the first year, and still {f.pinatuboTimes15} times less in the fifteenth, but every year. And it would only offset half of the rise in forcing from the start, {f.coolingYear15} °C less in the fifteenth year<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />. The slider runs through the {f.sliderMax} years.

The slider assumes the sulfur lofted grows by the same amount each year, as in the study, and that each flight carries a full load. The aircraft burns the sulfur in flight, which doubles its mass as SO2.

</ShowCalc>

## And the evidence of secret spraying?

A team put the most cited “evidence” to {f.experts} experts on contrails and atmospheric deposition: photos of trails, and tests of water, soil and snow. {f.expertsNo} of {f.experts}, or {f.expertsShare}, had never come across any sign of a secret programme. The single “yes” was about barium in the air at a remote site. The tests cited used thresholds meant for drinking water<SourceRef id="shearer2016" sources={sources} />.

A real programme would be seen: dozens of large aircraft, thousands of flights a year, from bases in several countries<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />. And aerosols high in the atmosphere have been measured by laser since {f.lidarSince}, then by satellite. These measurements already pick up eruptions of {f.volcanoMt} million tonnes of SO2<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />, about the size of the programme’s first year. An eruption releases everything at once, while a programme would spread its flights over the year: no study we read puts a number on the detection threshold in that case, and the strongest argument remains the number of flights.

</Step>

<Aside>

## And if it stopped all at once?

Masking warming is a long commitment. In {f.terminationModels} climate models where sunlight is dimmed and then the dimming stops abruptly, warming picks up, in the decade after stopping, about {f.terminationFactor} times faster than it would have without any intervention<SourceRef id="jones2013" sources={sources} />. That experiment dims the Sun instead of injecting aerosols; one model tested with aerosols gives a somewhat slower rebound.

</Aside>

## A Trail Is Ice

An aircraft engine releases hot gases laden with water vapour, and that vapour is what makes the trail. In air at {f.formation} °C or colder, it condenses into droplets<SourceRef id="schumann1996" sources={sources} />. Particles from the engine, such as soot, act as seeds, then the droplets freeze<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

In dry air, the trail is gone within {f.shortMinutes} minutes. It only lasts if the air is supersaturated with respect to ice. It can then hold for up to {f.persistentHours} hours and spread into a veil of cirrus. Aircraft fly through such air {f.supersaturated} of the time<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

The same moist air, lifted by a low-pressure system, also makes natural cirrus<SourceRef id="spichtinger2005" sources={sources} /><SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />. So lasting trails and cirrus often show up together, because they have the same cause.

</Step>

## Trails Warm the Climate Slightly

By day, a trail sends a little sunlight back to space. Day and night, it holds in the heat the Earth radiates, and at night nothing offsets it. Overall, the warming wins slightly<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />.

In {f.forcingYear}, contrails and their cirrus warmed the climate by about {f.contrailErf} milliwatts per square metre, within a range of {f.contrailErfRange}. That is more than the effect of the CO2 aviation has built up, {f.aviationCo2Erf} milliwatts per square metre<SourceRef id="lee2021" sources={sources} />.

The authors rate this figure as uncertain, but its sign is not in doubt. Spraying to cool the Earth that warms it would make no sense.

This ratio compares trails that last a few hours, renewed as long as planes fly, with a gas that builds up over centuries. It does not say which matters more in the long run.

</ShowCalc>

## Sulfur Cools, but Above {f.stratosphere} km

In {f.pinatuboYear}, the Pinatubo volcano sent {f.pinatuboSo2} million tonnes of sulfur dioxide above {f.stratosphere} km<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />. The sulfur forms fine droplets of sulfuric acid there, which send part of the sunlight back.

Up there, it takes about a year for their amount to drop by {f.aerosolDrop}. Lower down, rain clears them within a few days<SourceRef id="pitari2002" sources={sources} />. The Earth’s surface cooled by about {f.cooling} °C<SourceRef id="canty2013" sources={sources} />, for {f.coolingYears} years<SourceRef id="robock2000" sources={sources} />.

Airliners fly at about {f.cruise} km<SourceRef id="karcher2018" sources={sources} />, well below that. Whatever they released would come down within days to months<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

## A Real Programme Would Take Thousands of Flights a Year

No existing aircraft climbs to about {f.stratosphere} km with enough load. Research aircraft get there, but with about {f.researchPayload} tonne. So one study designed a new aircraft able to carry {f.payload} tonnes of sulfur up there<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />.

In its scenario, the first year takes {f.so2Year1} million tonnes of SO2, {f.flightsYear1} flights and {f.aircraftYear1} aircraft. The fifteenth takes {f.so2Year15} million tonnes, {f.flightsYear15} flights and {f.aircraftYear15} aircraft. On average over the fifteen years, it costs about {f.cost} dollars a year.

That is {f.pinatuboTimes1} times less sulfur than Pinatubo in the first year, and {f.pinatuboTimes15} times less in the fifteenth, but every year. The programme would only offset half of the rise in forcing, {f.coolingYear15} °C less in the fifteenth year<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />. The slider runs through the {f.sliderMax} years.

The slider assumes the sulfur lofted grows by the same amount each year, as in the study, and that each flight leaves full. The aircraft burns the sulfur in flight, which doubles its mass as SO2.

</ShowCalc>

## {f.expertsNo} Experts Out of {f.experts} Saw No Evidence of Secret Spraying

A team put the most cited “evidence” to {f.experts} experts on contrails and atmospheric deposition. It consisted of photos of trails and tests of water, soil and snow. {f.expertsNo} of {f.experts}, or {f.expertsShare}, had never come across any sign of a secret programme<SourceRef id="shearer2016" sources={sources} />.

The single “yes” was about barium in the air at a remote site. The tests cited used thresholds meant for drinking water<SourceRef id="shearer2016" sources={sources} />.

A real programme would be seen: dozens of large aircraft, thousands of flights a year, from bases in several countries<SourceRef id="smith2018" sources={sources} />. Aerosols high in the atmosphere have been measured by laser since {f.lidarSince}, then by satellite. These measurements already pick up eruptions of {f.volcanoMt} million tonnes of SO2<SourceRef id="kremser2016" sources={sources} />, about the size of the programme’s first year.

An eruption releases everything at once, while a programme would spread its flights over the year. No study we read puts a number on the detection threshold in that case: the strongest argument remains the number of flights.

</Step>

<Aside>

## Stopping Abruptly Would Restart the Warming

Masking warming is a long commitment. {f.terminationModels} climate models simulated a dimmer Sun, then a sudden stop. In the decade that follows, warming picks up about {f.terminationFactor} times faster than it would have without any intervention<SourceRef id="jones2013" sources={sources} />.

That experiment dims the Sun instead of injecting aerosols. One model tested with aerosols gives a somewhat slower rebound.

</Aside>

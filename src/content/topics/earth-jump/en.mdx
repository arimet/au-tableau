## When We Jump, the Earth Moves Back a Little

To jump, we push the ground down with our legs. The ground pushes us up, and the Earth gets the same push downwards.

The jumper and the Earth form a single system, whose centre of mass stays put. The jumper rises, and the heavier the Earth, the less it moves back.

The Earth is not a rigid block. The push travels through it as seismic waves, which do not reach the far side of the globe during the jump. The recoil we compute is that of its centre of mass.

On landing, the motion reverses: the jumper comes down and the Earth comes back up. Once everyone is on the ground, the Earth is back in its place.

</Step>

## The Earth Weighs Trillions of Times More Than Humanity

In 2021, we numbered {f.population2021} humans<SourceRef id="gbd2024" sources={sources} />. The question counts {f.people}, and we keep that number.

One team uses a mean mass of about {f.meanMass} kg per human, children included<SourceRef id="greenspoon2023" sources={sources} />. Humanity therefore weighs about {f.humanityMt} tonnes. The Earth weighs {f.massRatio} times more<SourceRef id="chen2015" sources={sources} />.

## Jumps on Opposite Sides of the Globe Cancel Out in Part

On a round Earth, up does not point the same way everywhere. Two jumpers at opposite points of the globe push the Earth in opposite directions, and their pushes cancel.

Only the imbalance in where humans live counts. Two economists worked out the demographic centre of the world: the mean position of its inhabitants, in three dimensions. It lies about {f.centreKm} km from the centre of the Earth, under Central Asia<SourceRef id="grether2010" sources={sources} />.

If humans were spread in equal numbers over the globe, this point would sit at the centre of the Earth, and every jump would cancel. Compared with the radius of the Earth, its distance gives the share of the jumps that remains: about **{f.kept}**.

</Step>

## An Ordinary Jump Moves the Earth’s Centre Back by a Few Femtometres

If all humans jumped from the same spot, a {f.jumpCm} cm jump would move the centre of the Earth back by {f.fullFm} femtometres. A femtometre is a millionth of a billionth of a metre.

Spread out as they are, the jumpers move the centre of the Earth back by about **{f.recoilFm} femtometres**, away from Central Asia. The {f.jumpCm} cm height is our choice, for jumpers of all ages.

The slider varies the jump from {f.sliderMinCm} cm to {f.sliderMaxM} m. The recoil follows the height of the jump: a jump twice as high moves the Earth back twice as far.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('recoil')} sources={sources} locale="en">

Grether and Mathys compute the demographic centre from the {f.bigCities} cities of over a million people between {f.sampleFrom} and {f.sampleTo}, not from the whole population<SourceRef id="grether2010" sources={sources} />. They count inhabitants, not kilograms.

And an adult weighs {f.asiaAdultMass} kg on average in Asia and {f.northAmericaAdultMass} kg in North America<SourceRef id="walpole2012" sources={sources} />. Our result therefore gives an order of magnitude.

</ShowCalc>

## Larger Than a Proton, Far Smaller Than an Atom

At the heart of the hydrogen atom sits a proton, with a radius of {f.protonFm} femtometres<SourceRef id="tiesinga2021" sources={sources} />. The Earth’s recoil is about {f.protonTimes} times that radius.

The whole hydrogen atom has a radius of about {f.atomFm} femtometres<SourceRef id="tiesinga2021" sources={sources} />. The Earth’s recoil is {f.atomTimes} times smaller. With a jump of {f.protonJumpCm} cm, the Earth would move back by one proton radius.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('scale')} sources={sources} locale="en">

The radius of a hydrogen atom here is the Bohr radius. The radius of the proton is that of its electric charge.

</ShowCalc>

## A Jumping Crowd Shows Up on a Seismometer

A seismometer {f.diazDistanceM} m from the FC Barcelona stadium records fans jumping after a goal. The stadium holds up to {f.diazGoalCrowd} people. Up to {f.diazConcertCrowd} spectators at a Bruce Springsteen concert there also made the ground shake<SourceRef id="diaz2017" sources={sources} />.

Close to the jumpers, the ground shakes far more than the recoil we compute, because the push stays local. These measurements concern one crowd and a nearby seismometer. We found no peer-reviewed publication that puts a figure on the effect of all humanity jumping on the ground.

</Aside>

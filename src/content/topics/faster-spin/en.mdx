## {f.hoursTarget}-Hour Days: {f.energyShare} More Rotation Energy

Today the Earth turns once on its axis in {f.hoursNow} hours. To do it in {f.hoursTarget} hours, it must spin {f.speedFactor} times faster.

That means adding {f.momentumShare} to its spin momentum, which physicists call angular momentum. Its rotation energy goes up by {f.energyShare}.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('target')} sources={sources} locale="en">

The calculation multiplies the rotation rate by {f.hoursNow}/{f.hoursTarget}. It shortens the sidereal day in the same proportion: one full turn relative to the stars, which lasts {f.siderealDay} today.

Aiming at exactly {f.hoursTarget} hours of solar day, from one noon to the next, would change the result by less than 1%. The torque is the one to apply without a break for a year.

</ShowCalc>

</Step>

## Rockets Fixed to the Ground Change Nothing

Picture rockets bolted to the ground, nose east. Each rocket pushes the ground east, but its gases leave westward and drag the air with them.

The air then rubs against the ground and slows it by the same amount. The atmosphere is part of the Earth: the momentum moves from one part to the other, and the total does not change. No number of rockets is enough.

A published calculation makes the same assumption: gases that fall back hand their momentum back to the Earth<SourceRef id="zhang2013" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

## Billions of Billions of Tonnes Would Have to Go West

Only mass that leaves the Earth can change its angular momentum. To speed the Earth up, that mass must go west, against the rotation. The higher the orbit, the more each tonne counts.

On an orbit {f.westAltitude} kilometres up, travelled westward, **{f.westTonnes} tonnes** give {f.hoursTarget}-hour days. The slider moves the target day from {f.sliderMax} down to {f.sliderMin} hours.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('west')} sources={sources} locale="en">

What the calculation leaves out. It assumes a launch from the equator and a circular orbit above it. It only counts the mass placed in orbit, without the rocket or its fuel. The tonne resting on the ground was already turning east with the Earth: by leaving westward, it also leaves that momentum behind.

</ShowCalc>

</Step>

## A Warmer Equator, Colder Poles

Suppose the Earth has been sped up anyway. Climate models simulate the air and oceans of a planet that spins faster.

At mid-latitudes, large eddies of air, the low-pressure systems, carry heat from the equator to the poles. On a faster planet, these eddies are smaller and carry less heat<SourceRef id="kaspi2015" sources={sources} />. So the equator keeps its heat, and the poles get less of it<SourceRef id="charnay2013" sources={sources} />.

When the spin slows down, it goes the other way: the tropics cool and the high latitudes warm<SourceRef id="navarra2002" sources={sources} />. One study tests days from {f.hoursNow} down to {f.jenkinsShortest} hours. Its author sees a large change in how the air circulates only below {f.jenkinsThreshold} hours<SourceRef id="jenkins1996" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

## The Mean Temperature Barely Moves

A model of the Earth {f.charnayAge} billion years ago compares {f.charnayDay}-hour and {f.hoursNow}-hour days. The mean drops by {f.charnayCooling} °C in a temperate climate, and rises by {f.charnayWarming} °C in a warm ice-free climate. Its authors conclude the effect is very limited<SourceRef id="charnay2013" sources={sources} />.

Clouds give no clearer answer. With {f.charnayDay}-hour days, one simulation finds {f.jenkinsClouds} fewer clouds<SourceRef id="jenkins1993" sources={sources} />. Another, which fixes the ocean temperature, finds slightly more clouds instead<SourceRef id="jenkins1996" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

<Aside>

## Clouds Cool Very Slow Planets

On a planet that turns once in a few weeks, a thick cloud layer forms on the day side. It sends back a large share of the sunlight<SourceRef id="yang2014" sources={sources} />. An early Venus could have stayed temperate if one turn took more than about {f.wayDays} Earth days<SourceRef id="way2016" sources={sources} />.

That is the opposite of the original idea, and the climate would change a lot. With {f.navarraStorms}-hour days ({f.navarraStormDays} days), mid-latitude storms almost vanish, and their rain with them<SourceRef id="navarra2002" sources={sources} />.

</Aside>

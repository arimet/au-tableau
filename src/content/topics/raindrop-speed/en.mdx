These figures hold at sea level and come from a formula fitted to laboratory measurements. Without air, a fall of a thousand metres would end at one hundred and forty metres per second. A raindrop is never shaped like a teardrop: big ones flatten as they fall."

## In Still Air, a Big Drop Falls at About Nine Metres per Second

In 1949, Ross Gunn and Gilbert Kinzer timed more than {f.dropsTimed} water drops falling through still air<SourceRef id="gunn1949" sources={sources} />. The largest weighed {f.maxMassG} gram, a sphere {f.maxDiameterMm} millimetres across. At that size, the drops became unstable.

Their measurements are still the reference for rain<SourceRef id="bringi2018" sources={sources} />. A formula fitted to them gives the fall speed for each diameter<SourceRef id="atlas1973" sources={sources} /><SourceRef id="ma2019" sources={sources} />. A {f.smallMm} millimetre drop falls at {f.vSmall} metres per second. A {f.bigMm} millimetre drop falls at {f.vBig} metres per second, or {f.vBigKmh} kilometres per hour.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('speed')} sources={sources} locale="en">

The diameter of the largest drop comes from its mass and the density of water<SourceRef id="wagner2002" sources={sources} />. The formula comes from Atlas and his colleagues, which we read in the paper by Ma and his colleagues<SourceRef id="ma2019" sources={sources} />. It holds in calm air, at sea level. Higher up, Bringi and his colleagues expect speeds a little higher than at sea level<SourceRef id="bringi2018" sources={sources} />.

The diameter is that of a sphere with the same volume.

</ShowCalc>

## With Size, the Speed Gains Less and Less

The slider changes the diameter of the drop, from {f.sliderMin} to {f.sliderMax} millimetres. From {f.smallMm} to {f.midMm} millimetres, the speed almost doubles. From {f.midMm} to {f.bigMm} millimetres, it gains only {f.gainMidBig} metres per second.

We stop the slider at {f.sliderMax} millimetres. Beyond that, the formula drifts from Gunn and Kinzer’s measurements, which stop at {f.maxDiameterMm} millimetres. Gatlin and his colleagues counted {f.seenDrops} raindrops: only {f.bigShare} were that big<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />.

## Without Air, a Drop Would Fall Far Faster

As it falls, the drop gains speed, and the air brakes it harder and harder. When this brake equals its weight, its speed stops growing<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />. Physicists call it the terminal velocity.

Without air, nothing would brake it. After a fall of {f.fallM} metres, a height we chose for the example, it would reach {f.vFree} metres per second. That is {f.vFreeKmh} kilometres per hour, {f.freeRatio} times the speed of a {f.bigMm} millimetre drop. Without air, a fall of {f.reachM} metres would be enough to reach {f.vBig} metres per second.

Bringi and his colleagues measured drops as a very turbulent storm passed. Those of {f.turbSmallMm} and {f.turbBigMm} millimetres fell {f.turbLow} to {f.turbHigh} slower than their terminal velocity, on average<SourceRef id="bringi2018" sources={sources} />. For {f.turbLessMm} millimetre drops, the gap was smaller.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('freefall')} sources={sources} locale="en">

Without air, the speed at the end of a fall is the square root of twice gravity times the height. We take gravity at {f.latitude} degrees of latitude, from the Geodetic Reference System 1980<SourceRef id="moritz2000" sources={sources} />. There it is {f.g} metres per second squared.

</ShowCalc>

</Step>

## A Raindrop Is Not Shaped Like a Teardrop

Loftus and Wordsworth point it out: a falling drop never takes the teardrop shape seen in drawings<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />. Small drops are spheres. As they grow, they flatten, then look like the top of a hamburger bun.

Their shape comes from a balance. Surface tension tends to keep the drop round, and the pressures of the air and the water deform it<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />.

Pruppacher and Beard studied drops in a wind tunnel. By their formula, the height of a drop is {f.ratioSmall} of its width for a diameter of {f.smallMm} millimetre, and {f.ratioBig} for {f.bigMm} millimetres<SourceRef id="pruppacher1970" sources={sources} /><SourceRef id="abhijeet2023" sources={sources} />.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('shape')} sources={sources} locale="en">

We read this formula in the paper by Abhijeet and his colleagues, who note that its slope changes from one season to the next<SourceRef id="abhijeet2023" sources={sources} />. For the smallest drops, the formula exceeds the ratio of a sphere: we cap it there. The drawing shows a flattened ellipsoid, the shape Loftus and Wordsworth use in their calculations<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />.

</ShowCalc>

## Past a Few Millimetres, a Drop Ends Up Breaking Apart

A drop breaks apart once surface tension can no longer hold it together<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />. In rain, collisions between drops often limit their size before that point. Very big drops still exist: about one in {f.hugeOneIn} reaches {f.hugeMm} millimetres<SourceRef id="loftus2021" sources={sources} />.

Gunn and Kinzer’s largest drops, at {f.maxDiameterMm} millimetres, were already unstable<SourceRef id="gunn1949" sources={sources} />. In rain, some grow further before they break apart.

## Before Lift-Off, the Rocket Already Turns With the Earth

The Earth turns once on its axis every day. At the equator, the ground therefore moves east at {f.groundSpeed} metres per second.

A rocket standing on its launch pad moves with it. Even before ignition, it has momentum. Physicists call this turning momentum angular momentum.

</Step>

<Step>
  <Figures slot="figure" name="gases" locale="en" />

## The Gases Hand Their Momentum Back to the Earth

Our first sketch told another story: the rocket pushes its gases, and the gases push the Earth. We got it wrong.

The rocket does push its gases backward, and the gases push it forward. But those gases stay in the atmosphere. The air slows them, then rubs against the ground and passes their momentum to the Earth.

The stages that fall back return theirs too. As long as everything stays on Earth or in its air, the total momentum does not change: it moves from one part to another.

</Step>

<Step>
  <Figures slot="figure" name="orbit" locale="en" />

## The Satellite Carries Momentum Away

In low orbit, {f.leoAltitude} kilometres up, a satellite circles the Earth at {f.leoSpeed} metres per second. The ground at the equator moves at only {f.groundSpeed}. The satellite also turns farther from the axis, and turning momentum grows with that distance.

So it has more momentum than at the start, and the extra comes from the Earth, which now turns a little slower. If the satellite falls back one day, it gives it back.

</Step>

## One Tonne in Orbit Adds Less Than a Femtosecond to the Day

One tonne placed in low orbit, {f.leoAltitude} kilometres up, makes the day longer by **{f.perTonne}**. A femtosecond is {f.femtosecond}. In geostationary orbit, higher up, the same tonne makes the day longer by **{f.perTonneGeo}**.

In {f.launchesYear}, an inventory of rocket emissions counts {f.launches} launches, failures included<SourceRef id="barker2024" sources={sources} />. We found no peer-reviewed publication that gives the mass placed in orbit. So the slider varies that mass, up to {f.sliderMax} tonnes.

What the calculation leaves out. It assumes a launch from the equator and a circular orbit above it, travelled eastward. It only counts the mass placed in orbit, without the rocket stage or the gases ejected outside the atmosphere. Like Zhang<SourceRef id="zhang2013" sources={sources} />, it assumes all the momentum of the gases returns to the Earth.

The day computed is the sidereal day, one full turn relative to the stars. The effect lasts as long as the mass stays in orbit.

## Tides, Earthquakes and Dams Weigh Far More

Tidal friction makes the day longer by {f.tidesYear} each year<SourceRef id="stephenson2016" sources={sources} />. According to a model, the {f.sumatraYear} Sumatra earthquake made it shorter by {f.sumatra}<SourceRef id="gross-chao2006" sources={sources} />.

The dams built from {f.damsFrom} to {f.damsTo} made it shorter by about {f.dams}<SourceRef id="valencic2026" sources={sources} />. The water they hold back makes the day longer, but the resulting fall in sea level makes it shorter by more.

To match the Sumatra earthquake in the opposite direction, we would have to place **{f.sumatraTonnes}** tonnes in low orbit.

</Step>

## No Known Effect on Temperature

We found no peer-reviewed publication that links a few femtoseconds to temperature. The known link runs the other way: ice melting under global warming makes the day longer by {f.climatePerCentury} per century since {f.climateSince}<SourceRef id="kiani2024" sources={sources} />.

One century of this effect is the same as placing **{f.climateTonnes}** tonnes in low orbit. Warming reaches {f.warming} °C on average from {f.warmingPeriod[0]} to {f.warmingPeriod[1]}, compared with the years {f.baselinePeriod[0]} to {f.baselinePeriod[1]}. It comes from human activities<SourceRef id="forster2024" sources={sources} />.

## Before lift-off, the rocket is already turning

The Earth turns once on its axis every day. At the equator, the ground moves east at {f.groundSpeed} metres per second.

A rocket standing on its launch pad moves with it. Even before it lights its engines, it already has momentum. Physicists call this turning momentum angular momentum.

</Step>

<Step>
  <Figures slot="figure" name="gases" locale="en" />

## Gases released in the air stay there

To gain speed, the rocket pushes its gases backward. In return, the gases push the rocket forward.

Gases released in the atmosphere stay there. The air slows them, then air friction on the ground passes their momentum to the Earth. The stages that fall back return theirs too. As long as everything stays on Earth or in its air, the total momentum does not change: it moves from one part to another.

</Step>

<Step>
  <Figures slot="figure" name="orbit" locale="en" />

## The satellite carries momentum away

In low orbit, {f.leoAltitude} kilometres up, a satellite circles the Earth at {f.leoSpeed} metres per second. The ground at the equator moves at only {f.groundSpeed}. The satellite also turns a little farther from the axis, and turning momentum grows with that distance.

So it has more momentum than at the start. This extra comes from the Earth, which turns a little more slowly. If the satellite falls back one day, it gives it back.

</Step>

## How much longer the day gets

One tonne placed in low orbit, {f.leoAltitude} kilometres up, makes the day longer by **{f.perTonne}**. A femtosecond is {f.femtosecond}. In geostationary orbit, higher up, the same tonne makes it longer by **{f.perTonneGeo}**.

In {f.launchesYear}, an inventory of rocket emissions counts {f.launches} launches, failures included<SourceRef id="barker2024" sources={sources} />. We found no peer-reviewed publication that gives the mass placed in orbit. The slider varies that mass up to {f.sliderMax} tonnes.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('one-tonne')} sources={sources} locale="en">

What the calculation leaves out. It assumes a launch from the equator and a circular orbit above the equator, travelled eastward. It only counts the mass placed in orbit: it ignores the rocket stage and the gases ejected outside the atmosphere. Like Zhang<SourceRef id="zhang2013" sources={sources} />, it assumes that all the momentum of the gases returns to the Earth. The day computed is the sidereal day, one full turn relative to the stars. The effect lasts as long as the mass stays in orbit.

</ShowCalc>

## Next to the tides, an earthquake and dams

Tidal friction makes the day longer by {f.tidesYear} each year<SourceRef id="stephenson2016" sources={sources} />. According to a model, the {f.sumatraYear} Sumatra earthquake made it shorter by {f.sumatra}<SourceRef id="gross-chao2006" sources={sources} />. The dams built from {f.damsFrom} to {f.damsTo} made it shorter by about {f.dams}<SourceRef id="valencic2026" sources={sources} />. The water they hold back makes the day longer, but the resulting fall in sea level makes it shorter by more.

To match the Sumatra earthquake, we would have to place **{f.sumatraTonnes}** tonnes in low orbit.

</Step>

<Aside>

## What about temperature?

As far as we found, no peer-reviewed publication links a change of a few femtoseconds to temperature. The known link runs the other way: ice melting under global warming makes the day longer by {f.climatePerCentury} per century since {f.climateSince}<SourceRef id="kiani2024" sources={sources} />. One century of this effect is the same as placing **{f.climateTonnes}** tonnes in low orbit. Warming reaches {f.warming} °C on average over {f.warmingPeriod[0]}–{f.warmingPeriod[1]}, relative to {f.baselinePeriod[0]}–{f.baselinePeriod[1]}, and it comes from human activities<SourceRef id="forster2024" sources={sources} />.

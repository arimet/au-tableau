## Removing About {f.shareToday} of the Sunlight Would Be Enough

Above the atmosphere, the Sun delivers {f.solar} watts per square metre<SourceRef id="kopp2016" sources={sources} />. The Earth catches this light like a disc, but spreads it over its whole sphere, four times larger. Each square metre therefore gets {f.perSquareMetre} watts on average. In climate models, {f.albedo} of it is reflected back to space<SourceRef id="kravitz2013" sources={sources} />.

In {f.forcingYear}, human activities exert a “forcing” of {f.forcing} watts per square metre on the Earth, compared with pre-industrial times<SourceRef id="forster2024" sources={sources} />. It weighs on the Earth’s energy budget as much as {f.forcing} W/m² of extra absorbed sunlight would.

By our calculation, removing about **{f.shareToday}** of the sunlight would offset it. Sunshade studies often aim for {f.doubling}, enough to offset a doubling of CO2<SourceRef id="sanchez2015" sources={sources} />.

<ShowCalc calc={calc('today')} sources={sources} locale="en">

This calculation sets two forcings equal: it gives an order of magnitude, not the output of a climate model. It leaves out the warming still to come from the energy the Earth is already storing.

It also assumes one watt less of sunlight acts like one watt of CO2 forcing, which is not quite true. With the full uncertainty range on the forcing, the calculation gives {f.shareRange}.

</ShowCalc>

## At L1, the Position Needs Constant Correction

To cast shade all the time, a sunshade has to stay between the Sun and the Earth. The L1 point lies on that line, about {f.l1} kilometres from the Earth<SourceRef id="sanchez2015" sources={sources} />. An object placed there goes round the Sun in one year, like the Earth, so it stays between the two<SourceRef id="bromley2023" sources={sources} />.

Closer to the Sun, an object would have to orbit faster. At L1, the Earth’s pull holds it back by the right amount.

But the balance is unstable. Without correction, the distance from L1 is multiplied by {f.efoldFactor} every {f.efoldDays} days<SourceRef id="angel2006" sources={sources} />. An object placed {f.driftFrom} kilometre from L1 drifts {f.driftTo} kilometres away in under a year<SourceRef id="bromley2023" sources={sources} />.

A sunshade orbiting the Earth would go round it, and would not stay in front of the Sun. It would take a whole ring around the Earth, an idea that has already been studied<SourceRef id="salazar2016" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

## Roger Angel Proposes Trillions of Transparent Discs

In {f.angelYear}, the astronomer Roger Angel worked out a sunshade made of small transparent discs, {f.flyerDiameter} centimetres across and weighing {f.flyerGrams} grams each<SourceRef id="angel2006" sources={sources} />. The discs bend the light by at least {f.deflection} degrees, enough for it to miss the Earth.

Blocking the light would cause a problem. An opaque screen would be pushed by it, like a sail, and its balance point would move towards the Sun, away from L1. Even transparent discs get a little of that push, so their cloud sits a little further out than L1.

To remove {f.angelShare} of the sunlight, Angel’s reference design places {f.angelFlyers} of them {f.angelDistance} kilometres from the Earth, in a cloud about {f.cloudLength} kilometres long. Their total area reaches {f.angelArea} square kilometres, for {f.angelMass} tonnes.

</Step>

## It Would Take Millions of One-Tonne Launches

To leave the ground, Angel prefers {f.launchers} electromagnetic launchers to rockets. Each one would fire a tonne every {f.launchMinutes} minutes for {f.launchYears} years. That makes {f.launches} launches. He puts the cost below {f.cost} dollars, for a sunshade designed to last at least {f.lifetimeYears} years<SourceRef id="angel2006" sources={sources} />.

The slider moves the share of sunlight removed from {f.sliderMin} to {f.sliderMax}%. The area, the mass and the number of discs follow in proportion.

## Moon Dust Does Not Stay in Place

In {f.bromleyYear}, three astrophysicists proposed another material: dust from the Moon<SourceRef id="bromley2023" sources={sources} />. It already exists, its grains are the size that casts the most shade, and the Moon’s pull is weak. The potential-energy gap between the Moon and L1 is only about {f.moonEnergy} of the gap between the Earth and L1. The dust still has to be sifted or milled.

But dust does not stay. Each grain spends about {f.dustDays} days in front of the Sun, then drifts away. Something like {f.dustPerYear} tonnes would have to be launched every year, {f.dustScenario} in their lunar scenario, with no end. The authors say so themselves: nobody controls a cloud of dust.

</Step>

## The Sunshade Leaves Less Rain and a More Acidic Ocean

To see what is left over, twelve climate models tested an extreme case. They offset a quadrupling of CO2 with a Sun dimmed by {f.g1Share}, far more than the share worked out above<SourceRef id="kravitz2013" sources={sources} />. Compared with the pre-industrial climate, the poles stay {f.g1Poles} °C warmer, and the tropics {f.g1Tropics} °C cooler.

There is also {f.g1Rain} less rain on Earth, and monsoons lose {f.g1Monsoon} of their rain over land<SourceRef id="tilmes2013" sources={sources} />.

Last, the sunshade removes no CO2 from the air. Ocean acidification goes on, and could even get worse, because a cooler ocean absorbs more CO2<SourceRef id="kravitz2013" sources={sources} />.

</Step>

<Aside>

## Its Author Rejects a Sunshade Race

Roger Angel says it himself: building ever larger sunshades so as to keep burning ever more coal, oil and gas would make no sense. His sunshade does not undo the chemical effects of CO2 either<SourceRef id="angel2006" sources={sources} />.

</Aside>
